\section{Custom Dataset Performance}
On the held-out custom test dataset, the optimised XGBoost model achieved 90.72\% accuracy, 94.6\% recall, 86.8\% specificity, 88.0\% precision, and an F1 score of 91.16\%. The recall indicates that most phishing URLs in this test set were detected, while specificity reflects the proportion of legitimate URLs correctly identified as non-phishing.\cite{bib1}

The results show a substantial performance decline on independent data compared with the custom test set. The average F1 score across the three datasets is 77.93\%. This supports the paper's central point: high performance on one dataset does not guarantee equally strong performance on another.\cite{bib1}

\section{Comparison with a Neural Network}
In the reported benchmark, XGBoost achieved 2.1\% higher accuracy, trained 12.6 times faster (0.95 seconds compared with 12.01 seconds), and used 3.6 times less memory. The neural network had faster inference (7.51 ms compared with 20.6 ms) and a smaller model size. The best choice therefore depends on whether training cost, inference latency, memory footprint, or predictive quality is the primary constraint.\cite{bib1}

\section{Impact of HTTPS}
The contribution of HTTPS presence differed by dataset. The reported accuracy change when the feature was included was +4.97 percentage points for the custom dataset, +0.04 points for GramBeddings, and +12.81 points for PhiUSIIL. The paper explains that the value of HTTPS as a signal depends on the distribution of legitimate and phishing URLs in each dataset. HTTPS alone should therefore not be treated as proof that a website is safe.\cite{bib1}

\section{Summary}
XGBoost performed strongly on the custom test set and was computationally efficient to train, but the independent-dataset results exposed a meaningful generalisation gap. Stable feature selection and cross-dataset evaluation are essential for estimating how a detector may behave outside its training environment.
