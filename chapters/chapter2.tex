\section{\MakeUppercase{Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs)}}

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) represent a major breakthrough in implicit representation for view synthesis. NeRF models a scene using a continuous volumetric function, where the color and density at any 3D coordinate and 2D viewing direction are implicitly defined by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). This approach introduces a differentiable rendering technique, trained with high-resolution multi-view images to minimize color consistency loss. This optimization results in photo-realistic rendering outcomes for novel viewpoints, as it prioritizes learning only the information necessary for image generation. Furthermore, NeRF's ability to leverage information-sharing across images means its performance typically enhances with an increased number of sharp scene images.

\section{\MakeUppercase{Challenges in Image Deblurring}}

A significant challenge arises when applying NeRFs to real-world scenarios where captured images often suffer from misalignment and blur artifacts due to movement. Directly training NeRFs with these imperfect images can introduce numerous artifacts. While initial research focused on simply deblurring each input image before training, this approach often fails to ensure consistent results because it doesn't effectively utilize the crucial characteristic of information from neighboring views in multi-view datasets. The Deblur-NeRF method addressed this by jointly optimizing blur estimation and image reconstruction using a deformable sparse kernel. However, an even more fundamental issue exists: increasing the number of blurry images beyond a certain point may decrease their utility, especially in practical applications like capturing light-field images where camera overlap introduces blurring errors and a persistent information imbalance between the edges and the center of the views.\cite{bib3}

\section{Camera Poses and Volume Rendering}
Camera extrinsics describe camera rotation and translation relative to the scene. NeRF predicts colour and density along camera rays; volume rendering combines these predictions to form an image. Consistent camera poses and adequate viewpoint coverage are important for reconstruction.\cite{bib8}
